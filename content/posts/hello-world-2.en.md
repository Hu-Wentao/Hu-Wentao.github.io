I once tried to create a blog on `mirror.xyz`. At the time, the promise of permanent data retention through IPFS excited me. It seemed to mean that a niche technology used mainly by geeks had begun to enter ordinary people's lives. But when `mirror.xyz` migrated to `paragraph.com`, many articles from the original blog disappeared completely. The supposed “forever” lasted only a few months.

This does not mean that IPFS was useless. `mirror.xyz` was a pioneer that gave ordinary people the possibility of “forever.” But the chain from creation to preservation to stable access for the final reader contains too many weak points. This leads to a disappointing conclusion: Web3 has not advanced much since Bitcoin was created. Many so-called innovations are built on fragile Web2 foundations. These small innovations, and even fake innovations, create the illusion that we are living in the future.
Perhaps this is what the saying means: “The future is already here – it's just not very evenly distributed”.

If unstable Web3 is often no better than Web2, it makes more sense to create a blog with Web2 and self-hosting. I therefore chose to host my articles locally and on GitHub, then archive them through the [Wayback Machine](http://web.archive.org/). This provides a more direct and lower-cost way to preserve data and prove its history.

I believe that when Satoshi Nakamoto created Bitcoin, the goal was not only a peer-to-peer currency, but also a trustworthy history. The smallest structure capable of powering a trustworthy historical system is a trustworthy transaction. I hope to see trustworthy transaction histories accepted more widely in the future. That consensus could ultimately reshape how we record, verify, preserve, and understand history.
